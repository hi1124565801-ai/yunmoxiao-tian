Independent research · Beijing and Guangzhou

Organized Variation: Replication and Customer-Created Experiences in a Chinese Bar Chain

What can a bar chain repeat when its customers make each night different? At Drunk N’ Jump (跳海), the invitation to take a shift, stage an event, or leave a message travels from one outlet to the next. What people do with it depends on who shows up, who already knows whom, and how staff organize the next occasion.

Yunmoxiao Tian · March 2025–November 2026
Supervised by Assoc. Prof. Wang Di, Department of Sociology, Peking University

Abstract

Before Drunk N’ Jump, a Chinese bar chain, opens a bar in a new city, it may already have recruited its prospective customers. Residents join a WeChat group, suggest locations, and sometimes help shape the space itself. Once the bar opens, customers can work behind the counter, organize performances, and leave drawings or messages for strangers. How does a commercial chain replicate a participatory format across outlets and sustain it over time when its operation depends on contributions it cannot prescribe? Bringing research on organizational replication (Winter and Szulanski 2001; Jonsson and Foss 2011) into conversation with studies of participatory organizing (Chen 2011; Riom 2024; Demetry 2025) and commercial nightlife (Mears 2015; Huang and Liu 2023), I examine Drunk N’ Jump through approximately fourteen visits to five outlets, twelve interviews with eleven people, and thirty publicly available posts and reports. By reproducing channels for participation, the chain draws on the skills, creativity, and relationships that customers bring from their lives beyond the firm, while employees retain authority over scheduling, event support, and the public circulation of customer expressions. Yet participation also reshapes the conditions under which these arrangements continue to operate: locally accumulated familiarity facilitates coordination while narrowing access for newcomers; unpaid training leads some participants to leave the labor role while remaining customers; and shifting interest requires managers to continually renew participation. I develop organized variation as a mode of organizational replication in which firms reproduce roles and arrangements for participation, while the contributions made through them vary across occasions and reshape the social conditions of subsequent participation. Replication in a participatory business is thus not a completed transfer but the ongoing work of sustaining a repeatable format amid changing relationships, capabilities, and commitments.
